Linear Regression vs. LASSO Regression

Swap Ridge's circle for a diamond, and something remarkable happens: the optimum starts landing exactly on the axes. This lecture explains why corners create zeros — and zeros create feature selection.

L1 Regularization Sparsity Feature Selection Sequel to Lecture 38 (Ridge)
0Problem

Ridge shrank everything. It selected nothing.

Recall the gene-expression scenario from Lecture 38: 10,000 features, 200 patients. Ridge Regression fixed the instability — coefficients no longer explode — but every single one of the 10,000 genes still has some small, non-zero weight in the final model. A biologist asking "which genes actually matter?" gets no answer. The model is stable, but not interpretable, and it still has to store and compute with all 10,000 numbers at prediction time.

What's needed now is a penalty that doesn't just shrink coefficients — it needs to eliminate the irrelevant ones outright, setting their weights to exactly 0, while keeping non-zero weights for the genes that truly predict the outcome.

⚠️
The unresolved question from Lecture 38Ridge leaves every feature "a little bit in the model." We need a mechanism that can decide a feature is out entirely — not just small.
1History

From geophysics to statistics

The idea of using an L1 penalty to recover sparse solutions first appeared in geophysics — Santosa and Symes used it in 1986 to reconstruct sparse seismic reflection signals. Statistician Robert Tibshirani formalized and popularized it for regression in his landmark 1996 paper, coining the name LASSO. Around the same time, the signal-processing community independently discovered the same idea as "basis pursuit." Three fields converging on one trick — penalize with absolute values, not squares — is a strong signal that the geometry underneath is fundamental, not a statistical coincidence.

2Engineering

What a sparsity-inducing fix must satisfy

Must still penalize largeness

Keep everything that made Ridge useful — big weights should still cost something.

Must create a geometric "attractor" at zero

Unlike Ridge, we now specifically want the optimization to be pulled onto the axes, where some coordinates are exactly 0.

Must remain a convex penalty

We still want a well-behaved, globally solvable optimization problem — no arbitrary non-convex cutoffs.

Can sacrifice smoothness at zero

Unlike Stage 2 of Lecture 38, differentiability everywhere is no longer a hard requirement — a sharp corner exactly at zero is now a feature, not a bug.

That last point is the pivot: Ridge's engineering constraints explicitly ruled out non-smooth penalties. LASSO's do the opposite — a kink at zero is exactly the mechanism that produces sparsity.

The one-symbol change that changes everythingRidge's penalty was ‖w‖₂² — sum of squares. LASSO's is ‖w‖₁ — sum of absolute values. Same "sum over coordinates" structure, but squares curve smoothly through zero while absolute values form a sharp V — and that V is what turns into corners in higher dimensions.

AnalogyRidge's leash (Lecture 38) let the athlete wander anywhere inside a circular field — smooth boundary, they stop wherever the fence and their sprint direction meet. An athlete sprinting toward almost any direction is statistically far more likely to get caught at one of those corner posts than to touch the fence's flat edge exactly — and a corner post on this fence means "zero" for one of the coordinates.
6Derivation — The Geometric Intuition for Sparsity

Why corners happen — and why corners mean zero

Just like Ridge, picture the elliptical error contours expanding outward from wML until they first touch the constraint region's boundary. The only thing that changed is the shape of that boundary — a diamond instead of a circle.

The elliptical contours reach the diamond's corner before they reach any flat edge — the tangent point lands exactly on the w₁-axis, forcing w₂ = 0.
1
Same ellipses as Ridge
The loss function J(w) hasn't changed — its contours are still ellipses centered on w_ML.
2
Different boundary shape
Instead of a circle, the constraint ‖w‖₁ ≤ Θ is a diamond with corners sitting exactly on the axes.
3
Corners stick out geometrically
A corner is a point of the boundary that "pokes into" more directions than any point on a flat edge — for most orientations of the ellipse, the corner is what gets touched first.
4
Corner ⟹ an axis ⟹ a zero coefficient
Every corner of the diamond lies exactly on one of the coordinate axes — meaning one coordinate of w is exactly 0 there.
📐
The precise reason, stated carefullyIt's not that corners are touched every time — it's that corners are touched for a much larger fraction of possible ellipse orientations and positions than any single point on a flat edge, because the diamond has non-smooth "extra exposed area" concentrated exactly at the corners. In high dimensions this effect compounds, and with many features, LASSO solutions are sparse (several axes' worth of corners, i.e. several zero coefficients) with high probability.
★Naming

Decoding "LASSO"

LASSO stands for Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator — and each word maps onto a piece of what we just derived:

The least-squares error term, unchanged from ordinary linear regression.
A

The penalty uses absolute values — the L1 norm — instead of squares.
S

Like Ridge, coefficients are pulled toward zero in magnitude.
S

Unlike Ridge, some coefficients are pulled all the way to zero — automatic feature selection.
O

It's a well-defined operator mapping data (X, y) and a hyperparameter λ to a coefficient vector w.

7Algorithm

Why there's no closed form — and what's used instead

Ridge had a clean closed-form solution (Lecture 38, Eq. 38.4) because its penalty is smooth everywhere. LASSO's penalty has a corner at zero, so ordinary calculus (setting a gradient to zero) doesn't directly apply. In practice, LASSO is solved iteratively:

# pseudocode — coordinate descent with soft-thresholding
function lasso_fit(X, y, lam, iters):
    w = zeros(X.shape[1])
    for t in range(iters):
        for j in range(len(w)):
            # compute residual ignoring feature j, then
            # "soft-threshold" — shrink toward 0, and clip to exactly 0
            # if the raw update falls inside [-lam, lam]
            w[j] = soft_threshold(partial_update_j, lam)
    return w

The soft_threshold step is the algorithmic heart of sparsity: any coordinate whose unpenalized update would fall within a small window around zero gets snapped to exactly zero, rather than merely shrunk.

8Optimization — Choosing λ

Same tuning philosophy as Ridge, different curve to watch

  • Cross-validation, exactly as in Ridge — sweep λ, pick the value with lowest validation error.
  • Watch the sparsity pattern, not just the error: as λ increases, plot how many coefficients are exactly zero. This "number of selected features vs. λ" curve is LASSO-specific — Ridge has no equivalent, since it never hits exactly zero.
  • LARS (Least Angle Regression) can trace the entire path of solutions across all λ efficiently, rather than refitting from scratch at each λ.
9Edge Cases

Where LASSO behaves unexpectedly

CaseWhat happens
Highly correlated featuresLASSO tends to arbitrarily pick one feature from a correlated group and zero out the rest — even if they're equally predictive. (Ridge instead spreads weight evenly across correlated features.)
More features than samples (D > n)LASSO can select at most n non-zero coefficients — a hard cap tied to the number of training points.
λ = 0Reduces to ordinary Linear Regression, same as Ridge at λ = 0.
λ very largeAll coefficients driven to exactly 0 — an intercept-only model.
10Pattern Recognition

Signals that should make you reach for LASSO over Ridge

You suspect most features are irrelevant You need an interpretable, short list of predictors Model needs to be fast at inference (fewer active features) Features are not strongly correlated with each other "Which variables actually matter?" is the business question

If instead the goal is purely predictive accuracy with many correlated, all-plausibly-relevant features, Ridge (or Elastic Net) is usually the safer default.

12Problem Families

The full regularization family, now complete

Ridge (L2) — Lecture 38

Circle constraint. Smooth shrinkage, dense solutions, handles correlated features gracefully.

LASSO (L1) — this lecture

Diamond constraint. Corner-seeking, sparse solutions, automatic feature selection.

Elastic Net

λ₁‖w‖₁ + λ₂‖w‖₂² — a rounded-diamond constraint region; sparse like LASSO but more stable under correlated features like Ridge.

Group LASSO

Zeros out entire groups of coefficients together (e.g. all dummy variables for one categorical feature) rather than one at a time.

13Expert Thinking

How a practitioner reasons about this day to day

🧠
Mental shortcut"Need interpretability or feature selection → start with LASSO. Need stability with correlated features → start with Ridge. Want both → Elastic Net." This single decision rule covers most real-world regularizer choices.
🧠
Mental shortcutAn expert never trusts LASSO's specific selected features too literally when predictors are correlated — the "winner" among a correlated group can flip between resamples. They check stability via bootstrap before reporting "these are the important features."

Common Mistakes

✗"LASSO always outperforms Ridge because it's sparse."
✓Sparsity aids interpretability, not necessarily accuracy — with many correlated, genuinely useful features, Ridge or Elastic Net often predicts better.
✗Assuming LASSO has a closed-form solution like Ridge.
✓The L1 penalty is non-differentiable at zero — LASSO requires iterative methods (coordinate descent, LARS), not a matrix inversion.
✗Treating LASSO's selected features as a definitive, stable ranking of importance.
✓Under correlated predictors, the specific subset selected can be unstable across resamples — validate selection stability before drawing conclusions.
14Exam Shortcuts

IITM-style traps and fast checks

🎯
Common trap question"Which regularizer sets coefficients to exactly zero?" — L1 / LASSO, because its constraint region has corners on the axes. L2 / Ridge's circular region has no corners, so it (almost) never does.
🎯
Fast sanity checkDiamond ⟹ LASSO ⟹ sparse. Circle ⟹ Ridge ⟹ dense-but-shrunk. If a question shows a rounded-diamond shape, that's Elastic Net.
🎯
ShortcutExpand the LASSO acronym cold under time pressure: Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator — "Selection" is the word graders look for when asking what distinguishes LASSO from Ridge.
15Industry Implementation

What this looks like in real tools

# scikit-learn
from sklearn.linear_model import Lasso

model = Lasso(alpha=1.0)  # alpha == λ, same naming convention as Ridge
model.fit(X_train, y_train)
# model.coef_ will contain exact zeros for unselected features

The R ecosystem's glmnet package popularized fast coordinate-descent implementations for LASSO, Ridge, and Elastic Net together, computing the entire regularization path across many λ values in one efficient pass — the industry-standard tool referenced across most applied statistics courses.

16Connections

Closing the Ridge–LASSO arc

Lectures 38 and 39 together form one complete idea, told through two shapes: a circle that shrinks smoothly, and a diamond whose corners create sparsity. That single geometric contrast — "does the constraint region have corners on the axes?" — is the lens through which every future regularizer in this course (Elastic Net, Group LASSO, and beyond) should be read.

🔗
Thread forwardThe natural next question — "can we get LASSO's sparsity and Ridge's stability under correlated features at once?" — leads directly to Elastic Net, which blends both penalties into a single rounded-diamond constraint region.
