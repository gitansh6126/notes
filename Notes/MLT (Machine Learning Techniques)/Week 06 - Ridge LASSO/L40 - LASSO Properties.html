Characteristics of LASSO Regression

LASSO's penalty has a corner exactly where we need to differentiate it. This lecture opens up the tool built for exactly that situation: the subgradient — and shows how it turns "undifferentiable" into "still solvable."

The suitcase story: one thread through Lectures 38, 39, and 40

Every section below refers back to this single story instead of introducing a new metaphor. If you remember nothing else, remember the suitcase.

You're packing for a trip

Each gadget you could bring is a feature. How much room and priority you give it in the bag is its coefficient wi. Packing well — bringing the gadgets that actually matter for this trip, at sensible sizes — is what "fitting a good model" means.

L37
No airline weight policy at all (Linear Regression)
No limit, so you throw in every gadget you can imagine needing, at full size, however unlikely. The bag ends up packed around last week's specific trip quirks, not this trip's real needs — heavy, unstable, unpredictable at the airport scale (overfitting).
L38
Airline adds a squared excess-fee (Ridge)
Now excess weight costs you fee ∝ (overage)² — a smoothly growing charge. To avoid it, you don't leave anything home; you pack a lighter version of everything — travel-size toothpaste instead of the full bottle. Every gadget shrinks a bit. Nothing reaches exactly zero, because shrinking an already-small item barely reduces a squared fee further.
L39
A different airline: a hard flat weight cap (LASSO)
This time there's no smooth curve — just a strict total-weight ceiling. Under a flat cap, the smartest packing strategy isn't "shave a little off everything" — it's "make deliberate calls." You decide the hair dryer just isn't worth its weight and leave it home entirely — its coefficient becomes exactly zero. Every zero is one fewer feature your final model even looks at.
L40
The knife-edge decision (Subgradients)
Picture the one gadget you're genuinely torn on — a phone charger you might need. Right at the exact boundary between "pack a sliver of it" and "leave it fully home," there's no single correct answer — a whole range of judgments are equally defensible at that knife-edge. That range of equally-valid marginal judgments, right at the zero/non-zero boundary, is exactly the subgradient set. You don't need one perfect rule for that ambiguous moment — picking any defensible judgment and reassessing the whole bag repeatedly still gets you to the best final packing list.
📚
Why this one story, and not four separate onesThe circle-vs-diamond geometry (Lectures 38–39) is the shape of the airline's fee policy. The subgradient (this lecture) is what happens at the exact gadget-inclusion boundary that policy creates. One consistent picture, not three disconnected metaphors.
0Problem

You've built LASSO's objective. Now who actually minimizes it?

Lecture 39 established why LASSO's L1 penalty produces sparsity — its corners on the axes. But it quietly deferred a harder question: Ridge's smooth penalty let us set a gradient to zero and solve a matrix equation directly (Eq. 38.4). LASSO's objective contains |wi| — and |w| has no defined slope exactly at w = 0, which is precisely the point most coefficients are trying to reach.

Gradient descent, in its ordinary form, needs a gradient at every point it visits. If the algorithm's whole purpose is to walk coefficients toward zero, and the function it's descending is undefined at zero, ordinary gradient descent breaks down exactly where we need it most.

⚠️
The precise obstructionf(w) = |w| has a sharp "V" at w = 0. Approaching from the right, the slope is +1. Approaching from the left, the slope is −1. There is no single well-defined derivative at the corner itself.

In suitcase terms: this is the exact moment of deciding whether the borderline gadget goes in the bag at all — and ordinary calculus has no single answer for a decision sitting exactly on that edge.

1History

Convex analysis to the rescue

The tool for this problem predates LASSO by decades. Convex analysis, developed heavily by mathematicians including R. Tyrrell Rockafellar in the 1960s–70s, generalized the idea of a "derivative" to work for convex functions that aren't smooth everywhere — functions with kinks, like |w|, but still convex (bowl-shaped, no false local minima). This generalized derivative is the subgradient, and it's the exact tool the LASSO objective needs, borrowed decades later by statisticians once L1-penalized regression became common.

2Engineering

What a fix for "no derivative at the kink" must satisfy

Must work at ordinary points too

Away from the kink, it should just reduce to the normal derivative — no special-casing the easy 99% of the domain.

Must give a usable descent direction at the kink

At w = 0 specifically, the algorithm still needs something to step in the direction of, or optimization simply halts there.

Must preserve convergence guarantees

Whatever "generalized slope" we use, it must still guarantee the algorithm approaches the true minimum for convex problems — no heuristic patches that can get stuck.

3Mathematical Translation

What property should a "generalized slope" have?

A normal derivative's defining property: the tangent line touches the function at x and lies below the function everywhere else (for a convex function). Generalize that: instead of demanding a unique tangent line, allow any line through the point that still stays below the function everywhere. At a smooth point, only one such line exists — recovering the ordinary gradient. At a kink, many such lines exist — and any of them is a valid subgradient.

4Formula Construction

Defining the subgradient

Subgradient Definition
f(z)  ≥  f(x)  +  gT(z − x)    for all z
Eq. 40.1

g is called a subgradient of f at x if this inequality holds for every possible z. Read it plainly: "the linear function built from g, anchored at x, never overestimates f anywhere."

f the (possibly non-smooth) convex function, e.g. |w|
x the point we're evaluating a subgradient at
z any other point in the domain — the inequality must hold for all of them
g a candidate subgradient (a vector — a scalar slope in 1D)
5Formula Anatomy

Why this inequality — not an equation — is the definition

PieceRole
f(x) + gT(z − x)The tangent-line approximation to f, built using candidate slope g, evaluated at any point z
≥ (not =)The defining requirement: this linear approximation must stay at or below the true function everywhere — never above it
"for all z"The strictness that makes this a global condition, not just a local one — g has to work as a valid lower bound everywhere, not just nearby
🧳
Back to the suitcasef(w) = |w| is the "how much do I still value packing this gadget" curve for the one item you're torn on. Away from the knife-edge, there's one honest answer for its marginal value — one tangent line touches without ever overstating the curve. Exactly at the knife-edge (w = 0, "pack it or don't"), a whole fan of equally honest answers all touch without overstating it — that fan is the subgradient set [−1, 1].
6Derivation

Working out the subgradient of |w|

1
Case w > 0
|w| is smooth here, ordinary derivative applies: subgradient = {+1}, a single value.
2
Case w < 0
Also smooth: subgradient = {−1}, a single value.
3
Case w = 0 — the interesting one
Any line through the origin with slope g ∈ [−1, 1] stays below the V-shape of |w| everywhere. Every such g satisfies Eq. 40.1.
4
Full subgradient set
∂|w| = {+1} if w>0, {−1} if w<0, [−1, 1] if w=0 — a whole interval of valid values exactly at the kink.
Away from the kink, exactly one tangent line stays below |w| (gold). Exactly at the kink, an entire fan of lines with slopes between −1 and +1 all stay below (purple) — each one a valid subgradient.

This is exactly why LASSO coordinate descent (Lecture 39) needs a "soft-threshold" rule instead of a plain gradient step — it has to handle this entire interval of valid slopes at zero, not a single number.

7Algorithm

Subgradient descent

Subgradient descent looks almost identical to ordinary gradient descent — the only change is that at non-differentiable points, you pick any valid subgradient (often the one with smallest magnitude) instead of the single gradient value.

# pseudocode — subgradient descent for LASSO
function subgradient_descent(X, y, lam, iters):
    w = zeros(X.shape[1])
    for t in range(1, iters+1):
        step = 1.0 / t   # diminishing step size — required for convergence
        g = subgradient_of_objective(w, X, y, lam)
        # at w_j = 0, subgradient_of_objective picks any value in [-lam, lam]
        w = w - step * g
    return w
💡
GuaranteeBecause the LASSO objective is convex, subgradient descent with a diminishing step size (like 1/t) is guaranteed to converge to the global optimum — even though the objective isn't smooth everywhere.

In suitcase terms: re-weigh your whole packing list over and over, using any defensible judgment at each borderline gadget — you'll still land on the best final bag, just after more reconsiderations than if every item's value were unambiguous.

8Optimization

Beyond generic subgradient descent

Plain subgradient descent works, but converges slowly (it doesn't monotonically decrease the objective the way gradient descent does on smooth functions). In practice, LASSO is rarely solved with vanilla subgradient descent — specialized methods exploit its specific structure:

MethodIdea
Coordinate Descent (Lecture 39)Optimize one coefficient at a time using the closed-form soft-threshold rule — sidesteps the need for a subgradient step entirely.
Iterative Re-weighted Least Squares (IRLS)Approximates the L1 penalty locally with a re-weighted L2 penalty at each iteration, letting each step reuse Ridge's closed-form machinery.
Proximal Gradient MethodsCombine an ordinary gradient step on the smooth squared-error term with a "proximal" (soft-thresholding) step on the non-smooth L1 term.
LARSTraces the entire solution path across all λ values directly, without needing to run an iterative descent at each λ separately.
∞Hyperparameter Behavior at the Limits

Study the extremes of λ, not one fixed value

λ → 0

The penalty term vanishes entirely. LASSO's objective collapses to plain squared error → identical to ordinary Linear Regression. No sparsity, no shrinkage.

λ increasing

More coefficients get soft-thresholded to exactly zero as λ grows → bias rises (model gets simpler), variance falls (model gets more stable) → the classic bias-variance trade captured in one dial.

λ → ∞

The penalty dominates completely → every coefficient is forced to exactly zero → the model degenerates to predicting a constant (the intercept-only model), regardless of what the data says.

🎯
Exam framingQuestions rarely ask "what is λ?" — they ask "what happens as λ increases?" or "what does the model reduce to at λ=0?" Always be ready to state both endpoints of any hyperparameter, not just its typical value.

In suitcase terms: λ is how strict the airline's weight cap is. λ=0 is no policy at all; λ→∞ is a cap so tight you're not allowed to bring a bag at all.

9Edge Cases

Where subgradient descent needs care

CaseWhat happens
Fixed (non-diminishing) step sizeConvergence is no longer guaranteed to the exact optimum — the iterate can oscillate around it indefinitely.
Objective value during descentUnlike gradient descent on a smooth function, the objective value can temporarily increase between steps — only the best-seen iterate is guaranteed to converge, not every single step.
Choosing which subgradient at a kinkAny valid g in the subgradient set works for the convergence guarantee, but different choices affect practical convergence speed.

13Expert Thinking

How practitioners actually reason here

🧠
Mental shortcutNobody hand-codes plain subgradient descent for production LASSO — it's the conceptual bridge that explains why coordinate descent's soft-thresholding rule works, not the tool you'd actually deploy.
🧠
Mental shortcutAn expert chooses a regularizer by first asking what structure they expect in the true coefficients — plain sparsity → LASSO; grouped sparsity (whole categories of features in/out together) → Group LASSO; correlated-but-relevant features → Ridge or Elastic Net. The regularizer is picked to match a hypothesis about the world, not chosen by default.

Common Mistakes

✗"The subgradient at a kink is undefined, so optimization must stop there."
✓The subgradient at a kink is a whole set of valid values, not undefined — any member of that set can be used to keep descending.
✗Using a constant step size in subgradient descent and expecting exact convergence.
✓Subgradient descent needs a diminishing step size (e.g. 1/t) for provable convergence to the exact optimum.
✗Assuming the objective value decreases every single iteration of subgradient descent, like it does in gradient descent.
✓It can temporarily rise — only the best iterate seen so far is guaranteed to converge to the optimum.
14Exam Shortcuts

IITM-style traps and fast checks

🎯
Common trap question"What is the subgradient of |w| at w = 0?" — Not a single number. It's the entire interval [−1, 1]. Students who answer "0" or "undefined" both lose the mark.
🎯
Fast sanity checkMemorize the subgradient inequality cold: f(z) ≥ f(x) + gT(z − x) for all z. If a candidate g violates this for even one z, it is not a valid subgradient.
🎯
ShortcutRidge = closed-form (smooth penalty). LASSO = iterative (kinked penalty). If a question asks which regularizer needs subgradients, it's always the one using the L1 / absolute-value norm.

15Industry Implementation

What actually runs in production

Production libraries essentially never run raw subgradient descent for LASSO — it's too slow to converge in practice. scikit-learn's Lasso uses coordinate descent internally (Lecture 39); R's glmnet uses a highly optimized cyclical coordinate descent across the full regularization path. Subgradient descent remains important as the theoretical justification that convergence is guaranteed at all — the specialized methods are faster instances built on that same guarantee.

# scikit-learn — solver choice is abstracted away from the user
from sklearn.linear_model import Lasso
model = Lasso(alpha=1.0, max_iter=1000)  # internally: coordinate descent
