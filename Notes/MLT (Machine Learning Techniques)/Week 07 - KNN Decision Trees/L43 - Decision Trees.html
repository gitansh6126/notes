Decision Trees

KNN asks "who's nearby?" every single time. A decision tree asks a short series of yes/no questions once, in advance, and then never needs the training data again.

◆ Recursive Partitioning ◆ Information Theory ◆ Greedy Optimization ◆ Model-Based Learning
−1

Cognitive Pattern

what kind of thinking is this?

Decision trees are built from three ways of thinking layered together. Recursive partitioning — repeatedly slicing a space into smaller, purer pieces. Information theory — measuring "uncertainty" with a real number so two possible questions can be compared. And greedy optimization — at every step, take the single best question available right now, without looking ahead.

0

The Problem

why does this exist?
🌳
Same schoolyard, new problem: the teacher got tired of re-scanning the entire yard every time a new kid showed up (that was KNN's whole weakness — see Lecture 42). She wants a short checklist she can pin to the office door: two or three quick yes/no questions that any substitute teacher could use to guess "Chess Club" or "Football Team" without knowing a single student personally.

This is what a decision tree buys you: a compact, reusable set of questions extracted once from the training data, instead of carrying the entire dataset around forever. Predictions become nearly instant — just answer a handful of questions and walk down the tree.

If you don't solve this, every prediction stays as expensive as KNN's: rescan everything, every time.

History & Engineering Thinking

motivation, before the maths

Why it was invented

Decision trees grew out of 1960s–80s work on automated classification (ID3, later C4.5 and CART). The goal was to mimic how a person actually reasons through a diagnosis or a decision: a short chain of targeted questions, not a single global equation.

Constraints before any maths

  • Each question must be simple: one feature, one threshold
  • Each question must have exactly two outcomes
  • Questions should be chosen to separate the classes as cleanly as possible
  • The whole checklist should stay short — the point was speed and clarity

Structure of a Tree

three kinds of nodes
FIG. 1 — the whole "model" is this tiny checklist. No training points are stored anywhere.

Root & Internal Nodes

Every non-leaf node holds one question — a feature compared against a threshold — with exactly two children, one per answer.

Leaf Nodes

Terminal nodes carry no further questions, only a final predicted label (here, +1 for Football or 0 for Chess).

Prediction — walking the tree

  1. Start at the root. Apply its question to X_test.
  2. Follow the Yes or No edge to the matching child.
  3. Repeat at every internal node reached.
  4. Stop at a leaf — its label is the prediction. No further computation happens.

Measuring "Goodness" — Entropy

from words to a number

A good question should split the yard into groups that are as "pure" as possible — ideally, all-Chess on one side and all-Football on the other. To compare candidate questions, impurity needs a number.

Step 1 — a pure group needs 0
If a subset is all one class, there's no uncertainty left — the impurity score should be exactly zero.
Step 2 — a 50/50 split needs the maximum
If a subset is split evenly, a coin-flip is the best you can do — this should be the highest possible impurity.
Step 3 — borrow from information theory
Shannon's entropy formula already has exactly this shape: it's 0 at the extremes and peaks in the middle.
Entropy(p) = − p·log(p) − (1−p)·log(1−p)
Formula · Entropy
Entropy(p)  =  − p log(p) − (1−p) log(1−p)
p fraction of positive labels (+1) in the set
1−p fraction of negative labels (0) in the set
log typically base-2, so entropy is measured in bits
FIG. 2 — entropy is 0 at both pure extremes and peaks exactly at p = 0.5.
Stage 6 · Why this exact shape The logarithm makes rare, surprising outcomes count for more than common ones — this is the same core idea used for measuring "information" more generally. Weighting each term by its own probability (p and 1−p) means the formula naturally averages the surprise you'd expect to encounter, which is exactly what "uncertainty" means.

Information Gain

comparing candidate questions

Entropy scores one group. To pick the best question, compare the entropy before the split against the combined entropy of the two resulting groups — the drop is the "gain."

Formula · Information Gain
IG  =  Entropy(D) − [ γ·Entropy(DYes) + (1−γ)·Entropy(DNo) ]
D the full dataset arriving at this node
D_Yes subset that answered "Yes" to the question
D_No subset that answered "No"
γ fraction of D that went down the "Yes" branch
Worked example — 8 students, two candidate questions Dataset: 4 Chess Club (label 0), 4 Football Team (label +1). Entropy(D) = 1 bit (perfectly balanced).

Candidate A — "Sports Interest ≤ 5?"
Yes: 3 Chess, 1 Football → p=0.25 → Entropy ≈ 0.811
No: 1 Chess, 3 Football → p=0.75 → Entropy ≈ 0.811
γ = 0.5 → weighted entropy = 0.5(0.811) + 0.5(0.811) = 0.811
IG = 1 − 0.811 = 0.189

Candidate B — "Sports Interest ≤ 6?"
Yes: 4 Chess, 0 Football → Entropy = 0 (pure)
No: 0 Chess, 4 Football → Entropy = 0 (pure)
weighted entropy = 0
IG = 1 − 0 = 1.0

Candidate B wins outright — maximum possible Information Gain means the split fully separates the two classes, so the algorithm picks it for the root question.

The Algorithm

greedy, recursive tree-building

Pseudocode

function build_tree(D):
  if D is pure (or stopping rule met):
    return Leaf(majority_label(D))
  best_question = question maximizing InformationGain(D)
  D_yes, D_no = split(D, best_question)
  left  = build_tree(D_yes)
  right = build_tree(D_no)
  return Node(best_question, left, right)
"Greedy" — the catch At every node, the algorithm takes whichever single question gives the highest Information Gain right now — it never looks two questions ahead. This is fast, but it can miss a pair of questions that together would have split the data better than either does alone.

Optimisation & Practical Details

keeping the tree useful

Stopping rules

  • Stop when a node is already pure
  • Stop at a maximum depth
  • Stop when a node holds too few points to split further

Pruning & alternatives

  • Prune back branches that don't generalise past training data
  • Gini impurity is a cheaper, similarly-shaped alternative to entropy
  • Ensembles (Random Forests) average many trees to fix greediness

Limitations & Edge Cases

where trees break
LimitationWhat goes wrongSchoolyard version
OverfittingA tree grown too deep memorises individual training points instead of general patterns.A 15-question checklist that perfectly nails every current student — but fails on the next new kid.
Greedy suboptimalityThe best single question at each step isn't always part of the globally best tree.The most useful first question might not be the one that looks best in isolation.
Unstable to small changesA slightly different training set can produce a very different tree structure.Swap out two students and the whole checklist might get rewritten from the root down.
Threshold-only splitsEach question only compares one feature to one number — diagonal or curved boundaries need many small steps to approximate."Is Sports ≤ 6?" can't express "is Sports+Music roughly balanced?" in one question.

Pattern Recognition — When To Reach For This

real-world identification

Good fit

  • You need predictions to be fast, at the cost of a slower one-time training step
  • Interpretability matters — the tree can be read like a flowchart
  • Features naturally suggest threshold-style questions

Poor fit

  • Boundaries that are naturally diagonal or curved
  • Very noisy data with a single tree (consider an ensemble instead)
  • Situations demanding a guaranteed globally-optimal split

Formula Shape Recognition

same idea, different notation

Entropy is sometimes written with a sum over all classes rather than the two-term positive/negative form used above — for binary labels these are identical: Entropy = −Σ p_c log(p_c) collapses to the two-term version once there are only two classes. Some texts also swap entropy for Gini impurity, Gini(p) = 1 − p² − (1−p)² — a cheaper, similarly-shaped stand-in that skips the logarithm entirely but still hits 0 at the pure extremes and peaks at p = 0.5.

Quick Recall

the whole lecture in one glance

A decision tree greedily picks, at every node, the yes/no question that yields the highest Information Gain — the drop in entropy from splitting the data. Unlike KNN, the result is a small reusable model; the training data can be discarded once the tree is built.
