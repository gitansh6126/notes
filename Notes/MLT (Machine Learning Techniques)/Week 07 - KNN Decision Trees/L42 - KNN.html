K-Nearest Neighbours

You don't need a model to make a prediction — sometimes you just need to know who's standing closest to you. This is the entire idea behind KNN, unpacked from first principles.

◆ Geometry ◆ Counting / Voting ◆ Pattern Recognition ◆ Instance-Based Learning
−1

Cognitive Pattern

what kind of thinking is this?

KNN blends three ways of thinking. It's geometric — every data point is a location in space, and "similar" literally means "nearby." It's a counting problem — once you've found the neighbours, you just tally votes. And it's the purest form of pattern recognition — there is no formula being fitted; the "pattern" is the raw arrangement of the training data itself.

0

The Problem

why does this exist?
🏫
The running example: it's the new kid's first day at school. They walk into the yard, and the two big friend groups are the Chess Club and the Football Team. Nobody has interviewed the new kid or scored their personality — but everyone can make a decent guess about which group they'll end up in just by looking at who they're standing closest to.

This is the real problem KNN solves: you have labelled examples, and a new, unlabelled example shows up — predict its label using nothing but its position relative to what you already know. No equation describes "chess-club-ness." The only information you have is geometric proximity.

If you don't solve this, you're stuck assuming every new point needs an entirely new, hand-built rule. KNN says: don't build a rule — just ask the neighbours.

History & Engineering Thinking

motivation, before the maths

Why it was invented

KNN traces back to early non-parametric statistics (Fix & Hodges, 1951) — a reaction against methods that forced data into a rigid formula (like a straight line). The idea was: what if the model made no assumption about the shape of the data at all, and just trusted local evidence?

Constraints before any maths

  • Must use the raw training examples directly — no compression
  • Must define "closeness" numerically
  • Must resolve disagreement between neighbours somehow
  • Should adapt automatically to how many neighbours "matter"

From Words to Formula

building the distance, step by step

"Closeness" needs a number. The most natural way to build one is to generalise the Pythagorean theorem — the same rule you used in school to find the length of a diagonal.

Step 1 — one dimension
If two students only differed in one trait (say, Sports Interest), distance is just the plain difference.
d = |xtest − xi|
Step 2 — two dimensions
Add a second trait (Music Interest). Now it's a right-angle triangle — Pythagoras gives us the straight-line distance.
d = √[(Δ Sports)² + (Δ Music)²]
Step 3 — generalise to n traits
A real dataset might have dozens of features. The same idea just keeps adding squared differences under the root, one per feature.
d = √[ Σ (Δ featurej)² ]
Formula · Euclidean Distance
d(Xtest, Xi)  =  √ Σj=1n ( Xtest,j − Xi,j )2
Xtest the new, unlabelled point (the new kid)
Xi one training point (an existing student)
n number of features describing each point
j the index running over each feature
Stage 6 · Why every part exists Squaring makes every difference positive (a −3 and a +3 gap should count the same) and it weights large gaps more heavily than small ones. Summing combines every feature into one number. The square root brings the units back to the original scale, so the final distance means "actual straight-line distance," not "distance², which is why it isn't just left as a sum of squares.
Worked example Sam (Chess Club) sits at (Sports 5.5, Music 3.5). Jake (Football, but currently tying his shoelace near the new kid) sits at (Sports 6, Music 4.5). The new kid is at (Sports 7, Music 5).

d(new kid, Jake) = √[(7−6)² + (5−4.5)²] = √1.25 ≈ 1.12
d(new kid, Sam)  = √[(7−5.5)² + (5−3.5)²] = √4.5   ≈ 2.12

Numerically, Jake is closer — even though Sam's whole friend group is a much better overall fit. This single measurement is exactly the trap that small values of K fall into, which the next section visualises directly.

The Algorithm

maths → executable steps
FIG. 1 — the entire KNN "training" happens at prediction time. There is no earlier fitting step.

Pseudocode

function predict(X_test, training_data, K):
  distances = []
  for each (X_i, Y_i) in training_data:
    distances.append( (distance(X_test, X_i), Y_i) )
  sort distances ascending
  nearest_K = distances[0 : K]
  return majority_label( labels of nearest_K )

The Hyperparameter K

not learned — chosen

K is never estimated from the data the way a slope or intercept is — you supply it. Watch what happens to the new kid's prediction as K grows, using the exact schoolyard layout below.

The two failure extremes K = 1 memorises noise — a single mislabelled or wandering point can carve out its own little "hole" in the decision region. K = N (the whole dataset) erases all local structure and just repeats whichever class has more total members, everywhere, for every query — as useless as answering every question with "whatever is most common overall."
Tuning · Stage 9 Because K trades off "sensitive to noise" against "blind to detail," it's chosen the same way you'd choose any hyperparameter: try several values of K, evaluate each on a held-out validation set, and keep the K that generalises best via cross-validation — never the one that merely fits the training data hardest.

Optimisation & Computational Cost

the price of laziness

Why prediction is expensive

KNN never compresses the training set into a formula. So every single prediction repeats the full distance computation against every stored point, then sorts the results. For N training points and d features, that's roughly O(N·d) work, plus a sort, per query.

Practical mitigations

  • Spatial index structures (KD-trees, Ball trees) to avoid comparing against every point
  • Approximate nearest-neighbour search for very large N
  • Dimensionality reduction, since distance grows less meaningful in very high dimensions

Limitations & Edge Cases

where KNN breaks
LimitationWhat goes wrongSchoolyard version
Outlier sensitivity Small K trusts every single nearby point equally, noise included. Jake just happened to be standing there — he isn't representative of anything.
Distance-metric choice "Nearness" is a modelling decision, not a fact — Euclidean, Manhattan, or a weighted metric all give different neighbours. Should "closeness" mean physical standing distance, or similarity in hobbies? These give different answers.
Computational cost No compact model exists — every prediction reprocesses the entire dataset. You must re-scan the whole schoolyard for every new student, every single day.
Lazy / instance-based learning There is nothing to "extract" — the raw data itself is the model, so it can never be thrown away. You can't send everyone home and just keep a summary note — you need the entire yard present for the next new kid.

Pattern Recognition — When To Reach For This

real-world identification

Good fit

  • Small-to-medium datasets where local structure matters
  • A fast, interpretable baseline before trying something heavier
  • Recommendation-style problems ("people similar to you liked…")

Poor fit

  • Very large datasets needing fast predictions
  • Very high-dimensional data (distances stop being meaningful)
  • Data with many irrelevant or unscaled features

Quick Recall

the whole lecture in one glance

KNN predicts a label by finding the K closest training points to a query and taking a majority vote. It learns nothing in advance — the training data is the model — which makes it flexible but slow to query and easy to confuse with a single wandering outlier.
